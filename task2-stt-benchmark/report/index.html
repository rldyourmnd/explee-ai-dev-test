Which transcriber actually hears our speech?

Five speech to text engines on the same 49 minutes of Russian technical talk, scored against a human transcript published by the conference itself, with the evaluation frozen before any engine ran.

Production recommendation: Whisper large-v3-turbo with a glossary prompt. Feed it your list of product and tool names. It recognises 61% of technical terms against 36% without, a larger gain than any difference between engines, and it is the only configuration that both wins the primary metric and clears the usability guardrail.

It carries one risk you must decide about. Prompted with ten plausible term names that are never spoken in the audio, it wrote two of them into the transcript in 5 of 99 segments, including replacing the real word Kubernetes with the invented Qdrantis. An engine that invents the term it was told to expect is more dangerous than one that misses it, because a reader cannot tell the invention from a fact. If that is unacceptable, run large-v3-turbo without a prompt: no inventions, and term recall falls to 36%.

Do not prompt large-v3. It has the highest term score measured here and it earned that by collapsing. On 19 of 99 segments it stopped transcribing and emitted a two word summary such as Кликхаус оператор. The guardrail catches this and rejects the configuration.

No engine is good at this speech yet. Even the recommended setup misses about four technical terms in ten. Keep a human in the loop on terminology dense meetings.

This is the employer's complaint, measured. Whisper writes a Latin term in Cyrillic letters, wav2vec2 dissolves it into syllables, SeamlessM4T drops the segment. Only Parakeet wrote Kubernetes here, which is why script sensitivity is scored separately from word error rate.

Why word error rate alone gives the wrong answer

WER weights ну and ClickHouse identically, so an engine can win on WER and be useless. The clearest case here is wav2vec2 XLSR Russian. Its WER of 0.785 looks merely poor, but its term recall is 0.000 and its Latin to Cyrillic rate is 1.000: it emitted no Latin script anywhere in 49 minutes, turning every English term into Cyrillic syllables. On WER it is last by a modest margin. On the question the employer asked, it is unusable.

Parakeet is the opposite trap. It has the best Russian only error rate in the field, 0.269, better than either Whisper, and the largest code switch penalty, +0.358. Judged on Russian fluency it looks like the strongest engine here. Judged on the speech this employer actually has, it is third.

The guardrail, and a change made after results existed

The frozen rule set a guardrail of WER 0.30, and every engine failed it by a wide margin. That was not five bad engines. It was the wrong measuring stick, for a reason we predicted when we adopted this corpus.

The reference is a conference transcript edited for publication: fillers removed, false starts cleaned, grammar smoothed. Every engine is therefore charged for words a human editor deliberately deleted, and that artefact lands entirely in the insertion term.

EngineInsertionsDeletionsRatio
Parakeet-TDT v30.4070.02218.5x
wav2vec2 XLSR Russian0.4190.03312.7x
large-v3-turbo, stock0.3490.0536.6x
large-v3, stock0.3510.0625.7x

Both rates are per reference word. Four independent models, three architectures, all inserting five to eighteen times more than they delete, in the same direction. They are not hallucinating in unison. The reference is missing words that were spoken.

So the guardrail metric changed, recorded as a dated amendment that states plainly that engine output already existed when it changed. The threshold number, 0.30, is untouched. What changed is what it applies to: substitutions plus deletions over reference words, which excludes insertions and therefore excludes the editing artefact, while still measuring what the guardrail was always for, how much of the reference an engine got wrong or missed.

The test of an honest amendment is whether it still fails things. This one rejects three of seven configurations, including large-v3 + glossary, which an earlier version of this report recommended. A threshold moved to rescue a favourite would not have failed the favourite.

How much can 49 minutes prove?

Before trusting any claim that two engines are equivalent, we simulated systems differing by a known amount and ran the same paired moving block bootstrap the real comparison uses.

True difference in term F1DetectedTrialsPower
3 points101208%
5 points2312019%
10 points5512046%
15 points8912074%

Differences below roughly 15 points of term F1 are not resolvable on this corpus. The two stock Whispers differ by 3.6 points, so they are unresolved rather than proven equal. The recommendation does not rest on that pair: the winning configuration beats stock large-v3 by 15.6 points, which is above the floor, survives Holm correction, and holds at every block size from 60 to 300 seconds.

Comparisons use a paired moving block bootstrap over contiguous 120 second blocks, 10 000 resamples, both systems drawing the same blocks. Segments of one talk are not independent observations, and an earlier version of this benchmark resampled them as if they were, which reports intervals that are too narrow. Correcting it changed no verdict, which is the reassuring outcome rather than the exciting one.

Why 99 segments and not 120. 2952.821 seconds divided by 30 gives 99 segments. Nothing was excluded, no segment was dropped for being hard, and no exclusion rule fired. An earlier corpus had 120 segments because it was a full hour; this talk is 49 minutes.

Reliability, measured. Every configuration returned output for 99 of 99 segments. Zero failed requests, zero missing files, across all seven configurations. The single empty transcript in the evidence above is one SeamlessM4T segment, not a request failure. The eligibility rule requires at least 98% coverage before a configuration may be ranked, and every one of them met it.

Fairness

  • Byte identical segment files for every engine, cut once. No engine received its own resampling, denoising or voice activity segmentation.
  • GigaAM's API refuses audio at 30 s and our segments are exactly 30.000 s. Trimming for that one engine would have broken the identical input rule, so it was not done and GigaAM is reported as blocked rather than quietly accommodated.
  • Raw output is stored and hashed before any normalisation. Normalisation is shallow (case, punctuation, ё, hyphens) and never rewrites one word into another.
  • All engines are open models on our own GPUs, so the audio was sent to no vendor, and the employer can re run this benchmark without buying anything.
  • Engines were run one after another rather than interleaved. On dedicated GPUs with no vendor side rollout the risk is small, but it is a deviation from the planned round robin ordering and is stated rather than glossed over.

Engines that did not run

  • NVIDIA Canary-1b-v2: NeMo 2.1.0 asserts inside its own canary2 prompt formatter, Expected the last token in answer_ids to be EOS. It needs a newer NeMo than the pinned image.
  • GigaAM v2: the 30 second ceiling above, and its long form path needs a gated voice activity model requiring a token we do not have.
  • Qwen3-ASR 1.7B: fails inside transformers 5.13 with Audio features and audio tokens do not match, tokens: 390, features: 390, comparing two equal numbers and raising. That looks like an upstream defect rather than a call site error.

Limitations

  1. One speaker, one talk, 49 minutes. Conference audio is cleaner than a meeting: no cross talk, no interruption, one voice. Results may not transfer to a four way call. An earlier corpus, a four host podcast, had exactly those properties but no independent transcript.
  2. Absolute error rates are not comparable to other benchmarks, for the editing reason measured above. Only the ranking and the term metrics travel.
  3. The glossary is this company's vocabulary. On this corpus term F1 is carried mostly by Kubernetes and ClickHouse.
  4. The full meeting track was not run. Every result here comes from 30 second requests, which cannot test drift over long context or duplication at joins. Segment level results may not transfer to long form use.
  5. Diarisation was out of scope and is not scored. The task asks which engine hears the speech correctly, not who spoke.
  6. Two engines short of the planned slate, each named with its blocker.
