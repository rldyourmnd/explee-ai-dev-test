Which transcriber actually hears our speech?

Five speech-to-text engines on the same hour of Russian technical talk, scored against a human transcript published by the conference itself — with the evaluation frozen before any engine ran.

Production recommendation: run Whisper large-v3-turbo. We recommend Whisper large-v3-turbo for transcribing your meetings. It is the engine to put into production today, on these grounds: it leads or ties on every quality metric we declared in advance, it is statistically inseparable from the only engine that scores higher, and it costs 1.7× less GPU time per hour of audio than that engine — 326.8 s against 565.7 s. Cost broke a tie that quality could not, and we say that plainly rather than dressing a cost decision up as a quality verdict.

The recommendation comes with one caveat we will not soften: no engine is good at this speech yet. The best term recall in the field is 0.40, so roughly six of every ten technical terms are still lost or mangled. Recommending the best available engine is not the same as saying the problem is solved — expect to keep a human in the loop on terminology-dense meetings, and revisit when the terminology-assisted track has been run.
The answer. Whisper large-v3-turbo. On the pre-declared primary metric it is statistically indistinguishable from Whisper large-v3, and the frozen tie-break resolves that pair on measured cost: turbo needs 326.8 s of GPU time against large-v3's 565.7 s for the same hour — 1.7× cheaper for quality the data cannot separate. Parakeet-TDT v3 is eliminated one step earlier, on code-switched-span error.

The caveat, stated up front. Every engine fails the frozen WER guardrail, and no engine is good at this speech. The best term recall in the field is 0.40 — six of every ten technical terms are still lost or mangled.

Why WER alone would have given the wrong answer

The complaint was specific: the engine hears РАКа for RAG and Lead House for ClickHouse. Word error rate weights ну and ClickHouse identically, so an engine can win on WER and still be useless — the terms are what a reader searches for and what changes the meaning of a decision.

So the primary metric is IT-term F1 over a 50-term glossary frozen before the corpus was chosen, with overall WER kept only as a guardrail. That ordering was declared in commit 9fd6ff8, before any engine produced a word.

The ranking below vindicates the choice: on raw WER the field order is nearly the reverse of the term ranking's top two, and the engine with the second-best WER has the worst term recall of the three leaders.

How the winner was chosen — the rule, not a judgement call

  1. Primary metric (term F1) cannot separate the top three. Whisper large-v3 vs turbo differs by −0.036 with interval [−0.143, +0.066]; vs Parakeet by −0.038 with [−0.140, +0.064]. Both intervals contain zero.
  2. Tie-break step 1, code-switched-span WER: separates Parakeet out (0.748 against 0.650 / 0.675).
  3. Step 2, Latin→Cyrillic rate: the two Whispers remain indistinguishable.
  4. Step 3, hallucination rate: still indistinguishable.
  5. Step 4, measured cost: 326.8 s versus 565.7 s of GPU time. Turbo wins.

The harness prints this verdict itself: "no winner: turbo and large-v3 are indistinguishable on every declared metric" — cost is what breaks it, and the report says plainly that this is not a quality judgement. If you already own idle GPU capacity, large-v3's point estimate is the higher one and either choice is defensible.

The Russian-only model is the control that proves the metric

wav2vec2-XLSR-53 Russian scored 0.000 term recall and a 1.000 Latin→Cyrillic rate: it emitted no Latin script anywhere in the hour. It is a Russian-specialised model, and it transliterated every single English term into Cyrillic — the employer's exact complaint, in its purest form. Its overall WER (0.785) is bad but not catastrophic, which is precisely how an engine can look merely mediocre on WER while being completely unusable for this speech.

Seamless-M4T-v2 shows a second failure mode: it condenses. It is the only engine that drops more words than it adds (omission 0.235 against insertion 0.112) — it behaves like a summariser, which is disqualifying for a meeting record.

The reference is independent of every engine ranked. That is the property that makes this benchmark meaningful: a reference built from the engines under test would measure agreement, not accuracy. The harness refuses that construction outright, and a test asserts the refusal.

The reference is edited, and here is the number

Conference transcripts are tidied for publication: fillers and false starts removed, grammar smoothed. That inflates raw WER for every engine identically, so the ranking stays valid while the absolute WER does not. A five- to eighteen-fold asymmetry in the same direction for four independent engines is not four engines hallucinating in unison; it is the reference missing words that were spoken. Roughly 30–40 % of each engine's WER is the editing, not the engine. Term metrics are barely affected, because product names survive editing — which is one more reason the primary metric is the term metric.

Every engine fails the guardrail, and the threshold was not moved

The frozen rule sets a WER guardrail of 0.30. Measured WER runs 0.578 to 0.785, so under the declared rule no engine qualifies at all. Much of that gap is the editing effect above. The threshold was calibrated for a verbatim reference and this reference is not verbatim — but it was chosen before the numbers existed, and adjusting it now is exactly the post-hoc tuning a pre-registration exists to prevent. So the verdict is reported as declared, the ranking is published alongside it, and the reader can see both.

Fairness

  • Byte-identical segment files for every engine, cut once, 16 kHz mono. No engine got its own resampling, denoising or voice-activity segmentation.
  • GigaAM's short-form API refuses audio at 30 s while our segments are exactly 30.000 s. Trimming for that one engine would have broken the identical-input rule, so it was not done and GigaAM is reported as blocked.
  • Raw output stored and hashed before any normalisation. Normalisation is shallow — case, punctuation, ё, hyphens — and never rewrites one word into another.
  • All five engines are open models run on our own GPUs, so the audio was sent to no vendor at all, and the employer can re-run this benchmark without buying anything.
  • Only the default track is reported. The terminology-assisted track is implemented but was not run, so no engine is compared with a glossary against another's default.

Engines that did not run, with the specific blocker

  • NVIDIA Canary-1b-v2 — NeMo 2.1.0 asserts inside its own canary2 prompt formatter (Expected the last token in answer_ids to be EOS); needs a newer NeMo than the pinned image.
  • GigaAM v2 — 30-second short-form ceiling as above; its long-form path needs a gated pyannote VAD requiring an HF_TOKEN we do not have.

Limitations

  1. One speaker, one talk, 49 minutes. Conference audio is cleaner than a meeting: no cross-talk, no interruption, one voice. Results may not transfer to a four-way call. An earlier corpus (a four-host podcast) had exactly those properties but no independent transcript, which is why it was replaced.
  2. 49 minutes, not 60. Stated rather than padded.
  3. Absolute WER is not comparable to other benchmarks, for the editing reason measured above. Only the ranking and the term metrics travel.
  4. The glossary is this company's vocabulary. Term F1 does not transfer to a company with a different stack. On this corpus it is carried mostly by Kubernetes and ClickHouse.
  5. Two engines short of the full slate, both named with their blocker.
