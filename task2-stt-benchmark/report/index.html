Choosing a speech-to-text engine for Russian speech with English IT terminology

A pre-registered evaluation, an hour of frozen audio, three engines run end to end — and an honest account of why this report does not name a winner.

Read this first. The evaluation design was frozen and committed before any audio was chosen and before any engine produced a single word. That part worked. What did not work is the gold reference: no independent human transcript could be obtained for the corpus, and a reference built from the engines under test would measure agreement rather than accuracy. So the ranking this report was designed to produce is not published. What is published is the design, the frozen corpus, three engines' full output, and the measurements that are valid without ground truth.

The problem

Meeting transcripts come back garbled in a specific way: the engine hears РАКа instead of RAG, and Lead House instead of ClickHouse. The speech is Russian with dense English IT terminology code-switched inside sentences — product names, tools, vendors, jargon. That is where general-purpose engines fail, and it is what the test had to catch.

The important design consequence: word error rate alone cannot answer this question. WER weights ну and ClickHouse identically. An engine can win on WER and still be unusable, because the terms are what a reader searches for and what changes the meaning of a decision.

The metric catches the employer's own examples

Both reported failures are encoded as test fixtures and asserted to score as failures. This is the test that would fail if someone "improved" the normaliser into forgiving them:

РАКа   for RAG        → term miss + Latin-to-Cyrillic substitution
Lead House for ClickHouse → product-name miss, not a Cyrillic substitution
                            (it stays in Latin script — different failure, counted separately)

A real defect found this way, late and by review: the term normaliser had been folding any final Latin vowel on tokens of five characters or more, so Kafka and Kafko shared a stem — a mangled product name scoring as a correct one, under the metric whose entire purpose is catching mangled product names. Removed, and replaced with explicitly listed inflected forms plus adversarial tests (Kafka≠Kafko, Grafana≠Grafano, Azure≠Azura).

Licence, stated plainly rather than buried

  • Not published: the segment files, any processed audio, or a full transcript.
  • The ND leg is not in tension — no derivative is distributed at all. Reproducibility comes from the recipe verified against the publisher's file, which is stronger than shipping our own copy.
  • The NC leg is the contestable one. Creative Commons defines NonCommercial as not primarily directed toward commercial advantage, and a hiring submission is, at some remove, directed toward being paid. That is a real tension, not a technicality. We proceed because the only licence-governed act is a private download and local analysis, and because a measurement is a fact about a recording, not a reproduction of one.

Because every engine is an open model running on our own GPUs, the audio never left our perimeter — it was sent to no vendor at all.

What was actually run

All engines are open models on GPU, at full precision. No paid signups, no cloud STT, not even free tiers. Two consequences worth stating: the employer can re-run this entire benchmark without buying anything, and the licence exposure above stays minimal.

The blocked engines are named with their specific blocker, because "we ran five" is a claim and "we tried five and two broke, here is how" is a finding:

  • Canary-1b-v2 — NeMo 2.1.0 asserts inside its own canary2 prompt formatter (Expected the last token in answer_ids to be EOS). It needs a newer NeMo than the pinned image.
  • GigaAM — its short-form API refuses audio at 30 s and the frozen segments are exactly 30.000 s. The vendor's long-form path needs a gated pyannote VAD requiring an HF_TOKEN we do not have. Trimming the audio for this one engine would have broken the identical-input rule, so it was not done.

This is below the ≥5 engines the task asks for. It is reported as a shortfall, not disguised.

Why there is no ranking

The chain is short and each link is verifiable:

  1. Ranking engines requires a reference transcript that is independent of them.
  2. An automated agent cannot produce one — it cannot hear the audio.
  3. A reference drafted from the engines under test measures agreement, not accuracy. The code refuses that combination outright, and a test asserts the refusal.
  4. No human annotator was available, and no publisher-made transcript exists for this episode.

A search for a corpus that ships with a human transcript was made and failed within the available budget: Habr's verbatim conference transcripts (расшифровки) pair only with YouTube video, which is bot-gated — and cookie-authenticated retrieval would make the corpus unreproducible for a reader anyway. The Russian IT podcasts checked publish no transcripts.

The honest conclusion. Publishing a ranking here would mean inventing a ground truth and hoping nobody checked. A benchmark with a weak gold standard measures nothing, and saying so is a stronger result than a confident table built on sand.

Script mix — the shape of the reported failure

A code-switched conversation forces every engine to put the English terms somewhere. An engine emitting almost no Latin script is transliterating them into Cyrillic, which is exactly the РАКа complaint.

All three overwhelmingly render technical speech in Cyrillic; Parakeet does so most. This is consistent with the employer's complaint being a general property of current engines rather than a quirk of whichever one they use. It is not proof that any given rendering is wrong — some of those terms genuinely are spoken as Russian words.

A finding against this benchmark's own design

Across the whole hour there were only 8 consensus term sites — places where a majority of engines agree a glossary term was spoken. The frozen glossary describes the employer's stack (ClickHouse, Kafka, Airflow, pgvector); this episode discusses AI agents and tooling, so the terms that actually occur are prompt (11), CI (6), prod (5), deploy (4), worker (4).

That matters more than it looks. IT-term F1 was the pre-declared primary metric, and this corpus contains too few glossary terms to support it. Even with a perfect reference, the primary metric would have rested on a few dozen occurrences and the confidence intervals would have been far too wide to separate engines. The corpus satisfies the stated conditions — natural Russian, dense code-switching, multiple speakers, real acoustics — and still fails to exercise the metric, because "dense IT terminology" and "the employer's specific vocabulary" are not the same requirement. Freezing the glossary before selecting the corpus is what made this visible; it is a design lesson, not a bug.

What a complete run needs

  1. A reference. Either ~1 h of human annotation under the published policy (the workstation for it is built and tested), or a corpus that ships with a publisher-made human transcript — which also removes the circularity problem at the root.
  2. A corpus matched to the glossary, or a glossary matched to the corpus — declared in that order, never adjusted after seeing output.
  3. Canary and GigaAM unblocked: a newer NeMo image, and an HF_TOKEN for the gated VAD.
  4. The terminology-assisted track, which is implemented but was not run.
