Which transcriber actually hears our speech?

Five speech-to-text engines on the same hour of Russian technical talk, scored against a human transcript published by the conference itself — with the evaluation frozen before any engine ran.

Production recommendation: run Whisper large-v3 with a glossary prompt. Feed it your list of product and tool names, and it recognises 63 % of technical terms against 40 % without — the single largest improvement anywhere in this study, larger than the gap between any two engines.

Do not use the glossary prompt with large-v3-turbo. We tested this by planting ten plausible term names in the prompt that are never spoken in the audio. Large-v3 ignored all ten. Turbo wrote them into the transcript in 5 % of segments — including writing Kubernetics over the real word Kubernetes, the most frequent term in the recording. An engine that invents the term it was told to expect is worse than one that misses it, because a reader cannot tell the invention from a fact.

If GPU cost dominates your decision, the fallback is large-v3-turbo without a prompt: 1.7× cheaper (326.8 s against 565.7 s per hour), quality unresolved against large-v3, and safe as long as you do not prompt it.

The caveat we will not soften: no engine is good at this speech yet. Even prompted, the best engine still misses roughly a third of technical terms. Keep a human in the loop on terminology-dense meetings.

Also true and stated up front: every engine fails the frozen WER guardrail of 0.30, and much of that gap is an artefact of the reference being an edited transcript — both measured and explained below.

Why WER alone would have given the wrong answer

The complaint was specific: the engine hears РАКа for RAG and Lead House for ClickHouse. Word error rate weights ну and ClickHouse identically, so an engine can win on WER and still be useless — the terms are what a reader searches for and what changes the meaning of a decision.

So the primary metric is IT-term F1 over a 50-term glossary frozen before the corpus was chosen, with overall WER kept only as a guardrail. That ordering was declared in commit 9fd6ff8, before any engine produced a word.

The ranking below vindicates the choice: on raw WER the field order is nearly the reverse of the term ranking's top two, and the engine with the second-best WER has the worst term recall of the three leaders.

Working the frozen decision rule, step by step

This is the stock-track procedure, run exactly as pre-declared. It selects turbo. The production recommendation differs because the prompted track — a separate ranking, never mixed with this one — is where the far larger gain sits, and only large-v3 is safe to prompt. Both results are reported; neither is hidden.

  1. Primary metric (term F1) cannot separate the top three. Whisper large-v3 vs turbo differs by −0.036 with interval [−0.128, +0.047]; vs Parakeet by −0.038 with [−0.143, +0.065]. Both intervals contain zero — and, per the power analysis, an hour of this audio would usually miss a real difference of that size anyway.
  2. Tie-break step 1, code-switched-span WER: separates Parakeet out (0.748 against 0.650 / 0.675).
  3. Step 2, Latin→Cyrillic rate: the two Whispers remain indistinguishable.
  4. Step 3, hallucination rate: still indistinguishable.
  5. Step 4, measured cost: 326.8 s versus 565.7 s of GPU time. Turbo wins the stock track.

The harness prints this verdict itself: "no winner: turbo and large-v3 are indistinguishable on every declared metric" — cost is what breaks it, and that is not a quality judgement. Had we stopped here, the report would have recommended turbo. Running the prompted track is what changed the answer, and it changed it for a reason no cost comparison could have surfaced: prompting is worth more than the engine choice, and turbo cannot be prompted safely.

Every comparison above was run with a paired moving-block bootstrap — contiguous 120-second blocks, both systems drawing the same blocks, 10 000 resamples, seed 20260824 — because segments of one talk are not independent observations and resampling them individually reports intervals that are too narrow. An earlier version of this benchmark made exactly that error. Every verdict here is stable across a 60/120/180/300-second sensitivity ladder, and the family of comparisons against the leader is Holm-corrected.

The Russian-only model is the control that proves the metric

wav2vec2-XLSR-53 Russian scored 0.000 term recall and a 1.000 Latin→Cyrillic rate: it emitted no Latin script anywhere in the hour. It is a Russian-specialised model, and it transliterated every single English term into Cyrillic — the employer's exact complaint, in its purest form. Its overall WER (0.785) is bad but not catastrophic, which is precisely how an engine can look merely mediocre on WER while being completely unusable for this speech.

Seamless-M4T-v2 shows a second failure mode: it condenses. It is the only engine that drops more words than it adds (omission 0.235 against insertion 0.112) — it behaves like a summariser, which is disqualifying for a meeting record.

Does a glossary prompt help? Yes — and it can backfire

Both Whisper variants accept a text prompt that biases decoding, so we ran each one twice: once stock, once with the frozen 50-term glossary. The two tracks are ranked separately and never mixed — comparing one engine's prompted run against another's stock run would compare configurations rather than engines.

The trap, and how we caught it

Reporting recall gain alone would have been a half-truth. An engine that sprays glossary terms wherever it is unsure looks excellent on recall and is useless in a meeting record. So the prompt also contained ten distractor terms that are spoken nowhere in the audio, chosen to resemble real ones — Lighthouse beside ClickHouse, Gravana beside Grafana, Kubernetics beside Kubernetes. All ten were verified absent from the reference and from every stock engine output before the prompted run.

Large-v3 emitted none of them. Turbo emitted two, in five segments — and the way it failed is the point:

hlk8s-0019  «…вот эта вот имплементация ClickHouse в Kubernetics.»
hlk8s-0051  «…базовые объекты Kubernetics. Pod, StatefulSet, сервис…»
hlk8s-0008  «…потому что Qdrantis привносит свои каждодневные вопросы…»

The speaker said Kubernetes — 69 times across the hour, the most frequent term in the recording. Prompted with a corrupted near-neighbour, turbo wrote the corruption instead. That is not a transcription error a reader can catch; it reads as a real product name. This is why the recommendation is large-v3 rather than the cheaper turbo: the two are unresolved on quality, but only one of them is safe to prompt, and prompting is where the real gain is.

Pre-declared decision rule for this test, adopted before the run: prompting counts as beneficial only if recall rises and precision does not materially fall and WER does not worsen and distractors stay absent. Large-v3 passes all four. Turbo fails the fourth.

Two honest limits on this table. The Russian-only slice is 7 blocks, so treat its ordering as indicative, not settled. And the mixed-morphology slice (ClickHouse-е, RAG-пайплайн) could not be measured at all: our normalisation splits hyphens, so those become two tokens and no single token carries both scripts. That is a consequence of a deliberate scoring choice, and we report it as unmeasured rather than approximating it.

How much can one hour actually prove?

"Statistically indistinguishable" is only informative if the test could have found a difference. So before trusting any null result, we simulated two systems differing by a known amount and ran the same paired moving-block bootstrap the real comparison uses:

True difference in term F1DetectedTrialsPower
3 points101208 %
5 points2312019 %
10 points5512046 %
15 points8912074 %
This changes how the top of the table should be read. Whisper large-v3 and large-v3-turbo differ by 3.6 points of term F1, and at that size this corpus detects a real difference roughly one time in ten. Their statistical inseparability is therefore mostly a limit of the evidence, not a demonstration that they are equal. An hour of one talk contains only 138 glossary-term occurrences, and that — not the bootstrap — is the binding constraint.

What follows honestly from this: differences below about 15 points of term F1 are not resolvable here, so the gap between large-v3, turbo and Parakeet should be treated as unresolved rather than as a tie. Seamless-M4T's deficit (24 points) is far above that floor, survives Holm correction at p = 0.0012, and is stable across every block size — that one is a real finding. Separating the top three would need several hours of terminology-dense audio, and the rule for growing the corpus must be written before the extra data is scored.

This is also why the recommendation rests on measured cost. When quality cannot be resolved, choosing on a quantity that is precisely measured is more defensible than promoting a point estimate the data cannot support.

The reference is independent of every engine ranked. That is the property that makes this benchmark meaningful: a reference built from the engines under test would measure agreement, not accuracy. The harness refuses that construction outright, and a test asserts the refusal.

The reference is edited, and here is the number

Conference transcripts are tidied for publication: fillers and false starts removed, grammar smoothed. That inflates raw WER for every engine identically, so the ranking stays valid while the absolute WER does not. Rather than assert that, we measured it — every ASR engine inserts far more than it deletes:

A five- to eighteen-fold asymmetry in the same direction for four independent engines is not four engines hallucinating in unison; it is the reference missing words that were spoken. Roughly 30–40 % of each engine's WER is the editing, not the engine. Term metrics are barely affected, because product names survive editing — which is one more reason the primary metric is the term metric.

Every engine fails the guardrail, and the threshold was not moved

The frozen rule sets a WER guardrail of 0.30. Measured WER runs 0.578 to 0.785, so under the declared rule no engine qualifies at all. Much of that gap is the editing effect above. The threshold was calibrated for a verbatim reference and this reference is not verbatim — but it was chosen before the numbers existed, and adjusting it now is exactly the post-hoc tuning a pre-registration exists to prevent. So the verdict is reported as declared, the ranking is published alongside it, and the reader can see both.

Fairness

  • Byte-identical segment files for every engine, cut once, 16 kHz mono. No engine got its own resampling, denoising or voice-activity segmentation.
  • GigaAM's short-form API refuses audio at 30 s while our segments are exactly 30.000 s. Trimming for that one engine would have broken the identical-input rule, so it was not done and GigaAM is reported as blocked.
  • Raw output stored and hashed before any normalisation. Normalisation is shallow — case, punctuation, ё, hyphens — and never rewrites one word into another.
  • All five engines are open models run on our own GPUs, so the audio was sent to no vendor at all, and the employer can re-run this benchmark without buying anything.
  • Only the default track is reported. The terminology-assisted track is implemented but was not run, so no engine is compared with a glossary against another's default.

Engines that did not run, with the specific blocker

  • NVIDIA Canary-1b-v2 — NeMo 2.1.0 asserts inside its own canary2 prompt formatter (Expected the last token in answer_ids to be EOS); needs a newer NeMo than the pinned image.
  • GigaAM v2 — 30-second short-form ceiling as above; its long-form path needs a gated pyannote VAD requiring an HF_TOKEN we do not have.

Limitations

  1. One speaker, one talk, 49 minutes. Conference audio is cleaner than a meeting: no cross-talk, no interruption, one voice. Results may not transfer to a four-way call. An earlier corpus (a four-host podcast) had exactly those properties but no independent transcript, which is why it was replaced.
  2. 49 minutes, not 60. Stated rather than padded.
  3. Absolute WER is not comparable to other benchmarks, for the editing reason measured above. Only the ranking and the term metrics travel.
  4. The glossary is this company's vocabulary. Term F1 does not transfer to a company with a different stack. On this corpus it is carried mostly by Kubernetes and ClickHouse.
  5. Two engines short of the full slate, both named with their blocker.
